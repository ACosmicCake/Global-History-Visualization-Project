How this is made

The Living Atlas places Earth and human history on a globe, each record pinned to where and when it happened. The records are written by AI models, not by historians, so read them as a well-sourced starting point.

Who writes the records

AI models draft each record from web pages they open, and another AI session reviews the draft before it is published. No historian has checked the records as a set. Automated tools check the structure of every record and whether the dates and figures it credits to a page appear on that page. They cannot prove that a claim is true.

The rules every record follows

  • At least two independent publishers, at least one of them an institution such as a museum, university, archive or government body.
  • Every date, place and number must come from a page that was actually opened. Gaps stay gaps.
  • Wikipedia counts as a source only for Featured or Good articles, cited at a fixed revision. Elsewhere it appears only as further reading.
  • Coordinates come from a named source, and every record lists its sources so you can check them.

How to read a record

  • Outline or full record. Most records are outlines: a short sourced summary and a pin. Full records add narrative, perspectives and media.
  • Confidence. Established, probable, contested or legendary, as the sources support it.
  • Least certain. The weakest part of the record, such as a date the sources disagree on.
  • Pins are often approximate. A person may be pinned at a birthplace and a long war at one city. Where the record gives a margin of error, the open record shows it as a ring on the globe.
  • Importance is an editorial judgement made while drafting. It decides which pins show when zoomed out, not what matters most.

Known limits

  • Coverage is uneven: some regions, eras and subjects have far fewer records, and English-language sources dominate.
  • Political borders come from Cliopatria and are simplified. They are approximate and often debated, and a blank area means no polity is mapped there, not that it was empty.
  • Ice-age coastlines show sea level falling on today’s sea floor, for the last 800,000 years. Ice sheets are drawn for the last 80,000 years, at a coarse one-degree grid, from a single reconstruction; other studies differ, especially before the last ice-age peak.
  • Prehistoric animations of impacts, eruptions, tsunamis and darkening skies are schematic, and say so as they play. The place comes from the record, and so does the size where the record states one; the motion is only an illustration.
  • Names on the ancient continents and oceans, such as Pangaea and Tethys, and the dates they show, are approximate.
  • Continents from 10 million to 1 billion years ago follow the MERDITH2021 plate model. Earlier globes are an artist’s impression and are labelled as such.

Found a mistake?

Every record has a “Report a problem” link that opens an issue on GitHub (it needs a free GitHub account). Reports are checked against sources and either fixed or answered with a reason.
